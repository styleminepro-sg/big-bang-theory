The beginning of our cosmic story

The Big Bang

The universe began in an extremely hot, dense state and has been expanding and cooling for about 13.8 billion years.

About 13.8 billion years ago

A universe in motion.

The Big Bang is the leading scientific theory for the origin of the observable universe. It says that everything we can see — galaxies, stars, planets, and even living beings — began from a much hotter and denser early state.

Space expanded from that early state. As it expanded, it cooled. That cooling allowed particles to form, atoms to appear, stars to shine, galaxies to assemble, and eventually planets and life to exist.

Important phrase: expanding and cooling. The temperature readout at the top left changes as you move through time.

Not an explosion in space.

It was the expansion of space itself. There was no empty room waiting to be filled, and no central point from which matter flew outward.

  • Not matter flying through pre-existing space.
  • Not a bomb with a centre.
  • Not the universe expanding “into” something.

Think of dots drawn on a balloon. As the balloon inflates, every dot moves away from every other dot, and no dot is the centre. Tap the box to try it.

One rule connects everything

Expansion makes things cooler.

As space expands, radiation stretches to longer wavelengths. Longer wavelengths mean lower energy, so the universe cools. Matter also spreads out, becoming less dense.

This simple idea explains why the early universe was a hot plasma, why it later became transparent, and why we can still detect a faint microwave glow today.

  • Smaller universe = hotter, denser.
  • Larger universe = cooler, thinner.
  • Today = about 2.7 K background radiation.

First fraction of a second

Rapid expansion

A very brief period of extremely rapid expansion is called inflation. In a tiny fraction of a second, the universe grew enormously, smoothing out large-scale differences.

Inflation also amplified microscopic quantum fluctuations. Those tiny variations became the seeds of galaxies and clusters.

Without inflation, it would be difficult to explain why the universe looks so similar in different directions.

After inflation

A hot particle soup

The early universe was filled with extremely energetic particles: quarks, gluons, electrons, photons and other particles constantly created and destroyed.

It was too hot for protons and neutrons to settle into simple matter for a while. As expansion continued, the temperature fell and particles could combine.

This stage helps explain why matter as we know it could only form after the universe cooled sufficiently.

First few minutes

First nuclei

It cooled enough for protons and neutrons to join and form the first nuclei: hydrogen and helium, with traces of lithium.

This period is called Big Bang nucleosynthesis. The predicted amounts of these light elements are one of the strong pieces of evidence for the Big Bang model.

  • Mostly hydrogen.
  • A large amount of helium.
  • Tiny amounts of lithium.

380,000 years

Light finally travels freely

Electrons joined nuclei to make neutral atoms, mostly hydrogen and helium. Before this, light kept scattering off free electrons, like fog.

Once atoms formed, the universe became transparent. The light released then is still observable today as the cosmic microwave background, cooled to microwave wavelengths.

This is one of the most important moments in cosmic history: the universe became see-through.

After the first atoms

The universe turns dark

There were no stars yet. The universe was filled with neutral gas, and the only light was the fading afterglow of the hot early state.

Gravity quietly began pulling denser regions together. Tiny differences from the early universe started growing into larger structures.

This period is often called the cosmic dark ages, because no luminous stars had yet formed.

100–200 million years

First stars

Gravity pulled gas together until cores became hot and dense enough for nuclear fusion. The first stars switched on.

These stars were probably massive and short-lived. They created heavier elements and released them when they died, preparing the universe for planets and life.

  • Fusion begins.
  • Light returns to the universe.
  • Heavier elements start forming.

A few hundred million years

First galaxies

Stars gathered into galaxies. Gravity bound stars, gas, dust and dark matter into large systems.

Over time, galaxies merged and evolved. The Milky Way is one product of this long process of cosmic structure formation.

Galaxies are not just collections of stars. They also contain gas, dust, dark matter, and black holes at their centres.

About 9 billion years

Our Solar System

The Sun and planets formed from a cloud of gas and dust already enriched by earlier stars.

Earth formed around the same time. The atoms in our bodies — carbon, oxygen, nitrogen, iron — were forged in stars or produced in the early universe.

This is why scientists often say: we are made of star-stuff.

13.8 billion years

Today

Today we see a universe full of galaxies, stars, planets and life. It is still expanding, and observations show the expansion is accelerating due to dark energy.

We look back across cosmic time: the farther we see, the older the light, and therefore the earlier the universe we observe.

  • The universe is still expanding.
  • It is still cooling.
  • Its large-scale structure continues evolving.

How do we know?

The Big Bang is not just a guess. It is supported by several independent lines of evidence that agree with each other.

Galaxies move apart

Hubble’s observations showed that, on large scales, more distant galaxies recede faster.

  • This relationship supports an expanding universe.
  • Rewind that motion, and everything meets at one point.
  • We now know cosmic expansion is a fundamental part of the universe’s history.
Cosmic microwave background

A faint afterglow from the early universe fills the sky.

  • Discovered accidentally in 1965.
  • Detectable in every direction at about 2.7 K.
  • Its tiny ripples are the seeds that grew into galaxies.
Hydrogen and helium

The early universe made mostly hydrogen, with a large amount of helium.

  • Their observed proportions closely match predictions.
  • The amounts of deuterium and lithium also support the model.
  • No other model predicts these numbers so precisely.

Questions people ask

Where is the centre of the Big Bang?

There is no special centre in space. Every region sees distant galaxies moving away, because space itself is expanding everywhere.

What did the universe expand into?

This question may be misleading. In the Big Bang model, space itself expands. The universe is not necessarily expanding into some pre-existing empty container.

What happened before the Big Bang?

We do not know yet. Our current physics breaks down near the earliest moments, so ideas about “before” remain speculative.

Is dark matter part of the Big Bang theory?

Dark matter is not the same thing as the Big Bang theory, but it plays an important role in how galaxies formed and how the universe evolved.

Will the expansion stop?

Current evidence suggests the expansion is accelerating. In the far future, the universe may become colder, darker, and more spread out.

The idea in one breath

  • The universe began hot and dense about 13.8 billion years ago.
  • Space has been expanding and cooling ever since.
  • It was not an explosion in space; it was the expansion of space.
  • Galaxy motion, the cosmic microwave background, and the hydrogen-helium mix all support the theory.
  • We still do not know what, if anything, came before.

The Big Bang is not just a story about the beginning. It is a framework for understanding how the universe evolved into what we see today.

What came before the Big Bang?

We don’t know yet. Science is still exploring the question.

Some ideas involve other universes, or a cycle of expansion and contraction. None of them are confirmed. The search is still going on.
